We study Kakeya sets in dimension five over two four-element rings: integers modulo four, and binary dual numbers. These are finite arithmetic problems with unusually concrete geometry. A line has four points. A Kakeya set must contain a whole line in every primitive direction. The target is to prove that ninety-four points are necessary; a construction already shows that ninety-four suffice. (Source: Kakeya_Research_Frontier_Round6.json.)

**Why the number ninety-four appears**

Reduce every coordinate modulo the ring's nilpotent element. The resulting residue space has thirty-two points, identified with the binary vector space F₂⁵. Above each residue sits another thirty-two-point space. A fibre is the collection of chosen points above one residue.

A standard construction fills one fibre completely and places two suitable points in each of the other thirty-one fibres. Its size is 32+2·31=94. The conjectural general formula is 3·2ⁿ−2. Proving this formula would require controlling configurations that distribute their points differently, including those without a full fibre.

Singleton fibres contribute no four-point line, so they can be deleted. The remaining fibres are called pair-active. Their residue locations form the support. Their sizes form a weight profile. These are different objects: the same list of sizes can be placed on many different supports, with many different shapes inside each fibre.

For each of the thirty-one nonzero binary directions, there are sixteen lifted ring directions to cover. Thus there are 496 directional demands. A residue edge can help only through pairs lying in its two endpoint fibres.

**What changed computationally**

Earlier resource bounds often counted what each fibre could contribute in isolation. The newer approach fixes the actual support and requires all incident edges to share one compatible set of fibre data.

The obstacle is shared geometry. A fibre might have enough total pairs to meet several separate numerical tests, yet have no way to place those pairs in all the directions demanded by its actual neighbours. Choosing a single affine space and a single maximal-pair subspace forces those demands to interact. This explains why fixing support geometry can eliminate cases that survive extensive collections of scalar inequalities.

At each fibre, the model chooses a realizable pair-count histogram, an affine direction space, and a maximal-pair subspace. For every residue edge it chooses directional pair counts. Exact sumset upper bounds limit that edge's contribution. Every binary direction must receive enough combined capacity to cover its sixteen lifts.

These are necessary conditions. A model assignment need not reconstruct a genuine Kakeya set. Nevertheless, if the necessary conditions are inconsistent, the corresponding Kakeya configuration is impossible.

There are thirteen relevant support classes with eleven active fibres and twenty-five with twelve, when a largest fibre is distinguished. The complete marked universe therefore contains thirty-eight cases. The classification includes tied largest fibres and every allowable placement.

The joint-maximum formulation also changes the organization of the search. Instead of fixing the largest weight separately in every run, it allows a whole interval, counts the hub inside the common point budget, and insists that every other fibre be no larger. One contradiction then rules out every maximum in that interval for the specified marked support. Tied largest fibres remain fully within scope.

**A short geometric explanation for one support**

One transparent eleven-point support is S=V∪W, where V and W are complementary binary subspaces of dimensions three and two. There are three nonzero points in W, seven nonzero points in V, and their common origin.

Call the three points centres and the seven ordinary vertices. Every centre-to-ordinary direction occurs on exactly one support edge. Such an edge must therefore cover all sixteen lifted directions by itself. This makes small fibres expensive for their neighbours.

Elementary pair bounds force every centre to contain at least sixteen points. The three directions seen from an ordinary vertex are independent; their pair graph is a union of three-dimensional cubes. Six required edges cannot fit on five selected vertices, forcing each ordinary fibre to contain at least six points. The origin contributes at least two.

The resulting baseline is 3·16+7·6+2=92. A hypothetical ninety-three-point counterexample has only one point of slack. At least two centres therefore have exactly sixteen points, and many adjacent ordinary fibres have exactly six.

Those neighbours force maximal pairings in enough independent directions to make each sixteen-point centre an affine four-dimensional flat. Its pair counts vanish outside its direction space. Every edge joining centres consequently loses its contribution. The origin, with at most three points, cannot replace the missing coverage.

This proves the displayed support impossible without any maximum-fibre assumption, spectrum census, or SAT argument. (Source: SUPPORT11_COMPLEMENTARY_SUBSPACES_PROOF.md.)

**A new lemma about almost-periodicity**

For a fibre A, write p_A(d) for its number of pairs in direction d. Let K contain zero and every direction attaining the largest possible count, floor(|A|/2). These directions form a subspace.

For even |A|, K consists of translation periods. The odd case has a useful additional description: **if K is nontrivial, A differs by one point from a K-invariant set.**

Here is the argument. Put h=|K| and let t_C count the points of A in a K-coset C. Every nonzero maximal direction leaves exactly one point unmatched. Counting those deficits over K gives Σ_C t_C(h−t_C)=h−1. Every partially occupied coset contributes at least h−1. Therefore exactly one coset is partial, containing either one point or all but one; every other coset is empty or full.

It follows that p_A(d)=p_A(e) whenever d,e are nonzero and d+e belongs to K. Pair counts must agree across each outside coset of K. Also K lies inside the fibre's affine direction space.

We implemented these additional constraints. A bounded comparison excluded one marked support-thirteen case, with both baseline and strengthened formulas independently accepted. That is one excluded case, not two. The comparison showed no solver-speed advantage from the extra constraints; another selected case timed out under both formulas.
