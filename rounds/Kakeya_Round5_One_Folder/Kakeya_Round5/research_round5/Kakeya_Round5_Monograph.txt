**Kakeya Round5: a support theorem and a clearer route forward**

**The question**

A Kakeya set contains a line in every direction. Here the ambient space
is five dimensional over one of two four-element rings: integers modulo
four, or binary dual numbers. Each relevant line has four points. Our
target is an exact minimum of 94 points, the dimension-five instance of
the proposed formula 3 times two to the dimension, minus two. **The full
lower bound remains open.** What we have proved in this round is a new
structural restriction on any hypothetical smaller example.

Reduce every coordinate modulo the ring's two-element residue field.
The resulting space has 32 residue locations. Above each location sits
a fibre containing at most 32 points. A fibre with fewer than two points
cannot contribute to one of the primitive four-point lines. We therefore
call fibres containing at least two points pair-active. Our new theorem
says that a Kakeya set with at most 93 points must occupy at least eleven
pair-active residue locations. The difficult ten-location branch is now
excluded completely, **with no assumption on the largest fibre.**

**What changed elsewhere**

The other session reports that all 38 maximum-19 cases on the inherited
open supports have verified DRAT refutations. We have moved our research
priority to maxima eleven through eighteen. We have not received those
38 original CNFs and proof files, so their independent acceptance remains
an explicit task. What we did establish independently is the complete
case universe: thirteen marked support classes of size eleven and
twenty-five of size twelve. The reported count is exactly right.

This distinction matters. A proof checker can establish that a particular
Boolean formula is unsatisfiable. A mathematical argument must establish
that every hypothetical Kakeya counterexample would satisfy that formula.
Both links are necessary. Keeping them separate makes the conclusion
stronger and tells us precisely what remains to be checked.

**The geometry behind ten locations**

Every nonzero residue direction must occur between two active locations.
A ten-point support must therefore cover all 31 nonzero differences in
the binary five-dimensional space. We classified these supports directly.
Normalize an affine basis, then inspect the 14,950 possible extensions
by four more points. Exactly eighty normalized supports work, and all
have the same affine structure. A separate implementation verifies every
case without relying on the previous orbit catalogue.

The structure has three centres and seven ordinary vertices. It is the
union of the nonzero points of complementary subspaces of dimensions
two and three. Every edge touching a centre is the only support edge
in its direction. There are twenty-four such unique edges. The other
seven directions each occur on three ordinary edges. Uniqueness turns
an aggregate coverage requirement into a compulsory obligation at each
endpoint, which is the main reason this support is tractable.

**Counting pairs instead of guessing shapes**

For each direction, count the disjoint pairs inside a fibre. Across a
unique support edge, the two endpoint pair sets must cover a sixteen-point
quotient by addition. Their sizes must have product at least sixteen.
There is also a sharper elementary observation: if one pair set has
three elements, the other must have at least eight. Three binary points
span a four-point plane, and each of its four quotient cosets needs at
least two points from the other summand.

The incident unique directions at each vertex are sum-free. Consequently
the corresponding graph inside a fibre is triangle-free. Mantel's theorem
bounds its pairs by the square of the fibre weight divided by four,
rounded down. At an ordinary vertex the three directions are independent,
so its graph consists of disjoint three-dimensional cubes. Five points
in these cubes supply at most five pairs. Three unique edges demanding
two pairs each therefore cannot be supported by a five-point fibre.

**Why the centres must be large**

Let a be the smallest central weight. If a is at most seven, every
ordinary neighbour needs at least sixteen points, immediately exceeding
our total budget. If a is between eight and eleven, every ordinary
weight is at least eight. At most one of the centre's nine neighbours
can then have weight at least sixteen. Eight neighbours demand four
central pairs each, and the possible larger neighbour still demands two.
That requires at least 34 pairs, exceeding the largest available Mantel
budget of 30. Every centre therefore has weight at least twelve.

If the smallest centre has weight between twelve and fifteen, the seven
ordinary fibres each have weight at least eight. The central total is
at most 37, forcing a twelve-point centre whose neighbours have weight
at most thirteen. Each of its nine directions requires four pairs.
The total reaches 36, exactly Mantel's equality threshold.

That equality would require a complete bipartite graph with six vertices
on each side. But the actual direction geometry forbids it: any six
candidate opposite vertices have at most two common neighbours. This
has a short analytic proof, independently corroborated by checking all
eighty-four six-subsets. The entire low-centre branch is excluded.

**Only three extra points remain**

The remaining centres all have weight at least sixteen. A full thirty-two
point centre requires separate treatment. Two such centres already force
at least 94 points. One full centre forces ordinary fibres to supply
at least five pairs in their three cube directions, hence to contain
at least five points. The total would then be at least 99. Thus no
centre is full, and every ordinary edge requires two endpoint pairs.

The cube bound now forces every ordinary weight to be at least six.
Three centres of weight sixteen and seven ordinary fibres of weight
six already use ninety points. **There are at most three points left.**
If a centre has weight sixteen, at least four ordinary neighbours
still have weight exactly six. Each forces eight central pairs in its
direction. Those eight pairs exhaust the central fibre, making that
direction a period of the whole fibre.

Four distinct nonzero periods span at least three dimensions. A sixteen-point
set with such a period space is two eight-point cosets, hence an affine
four-dimensional flat. Its positive pair directions lie in four dimensions.
The centre's required unique directions span five dimensions. This is
the contradiction that closes every branch containing a sixteen-point centre.

**The last possibility**

Without a sixteen-point centre, all three centres have weight at least
seventeen. The entire budget is exhausted: their weights must be seventeen,
and all seven ordinary weights must be six. A central fibre then has
eight pairs in each of seven directions, for 56 pairs altogether.

Those directions lie in a four-dimensional subspace. Inside either of
its two cosets, the graph is the complete bipartite graph on eight plus
eight vertices with a perfect matching removed. Elementary degree and
triangle-free bounds show that any distribution of seventeen points
other than sixteen plus one supplies at most 53 pairs. The fibre must
therefore be a full four-flat plus one exterior point. Outside that
flat it has only one pair per direction, too few for its remaining
unique edges. The final branch is impossible.

**Why the two rings still matter**

The exclusions above work for both rings because carry translates
a edge's quotient sumset without changing its size or whether
it fills the quotient. That does not mean the two global problems are
identical. In larger supports, several edges can share responsibility
for one direction, while the same fibre must participate consistently
in many directions. Their translated label sets can overlap differently.
A relaxation that remembers only capacities can miss those global
constraints. Our next models should preserve the labels and
the shared fibre choices whenever those distinctions affect feasibility.

The main strategic gain is therefore structural. We have replaced a
large collection of sparse-support numerical survivors with one theorem
that excludes every placement and every fibre shape in that branch.
The remaining problem is substantial, but its boundary is sharper.
A successful continuation should seek similarly reusable restrictions,
then certify the residual cases with a complete encoding
and accepted proofs.

**What the result means**

The weight argument is now analytic; it does not depend on an exhaustive
weight-profile search. We nevertheless checked all 102,842 total-93 profiles
with weights at most eighteen through two different enumeration methods.
Their eleven residual profiles are eliminated by the same rigidity arguments.
The finite support classification remains a stated dependency. The complete
theorem has not been formalized in a proof assistant.

Across the middle ledger, this round removes 4,976 additional weight
profiles. All 3,220 previous support-ten survivors disappear. We retain
270,779 necessary-condition profiles in 48 support/maximum cells, with
supports now beginning at eleven. These numbers describe weight multisets,
not actual constructions and not the other session's SAT case inventory.

We also compiled fifteen Lean theorems connecting ring coordinates,
carry, four-point lines, and exact pair-sum witnesses. A separate pipeline
checks archived proofs through the upstream verified CakeML checker.
It does not certify the unseen new proofs or the unseen encoder.
The next priority is to combine that acceptance path with the ongoing
middle sweep and extend the geometric constraints to larger supports.
