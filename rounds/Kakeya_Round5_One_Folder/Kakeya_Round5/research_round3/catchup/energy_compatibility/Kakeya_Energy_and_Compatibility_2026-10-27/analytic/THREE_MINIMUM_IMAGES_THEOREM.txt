THREE MINIMUM IMAGES SUFFICE FOR THE SHARP LENGTH-TWO KAKEYA BOUND
27 October 2026. Analytic theorem; no unrestricted lower-bound claim.

Setup. R is Z/4Z or F2[e]/(e^2), pi is 2 or e, G=R^n, N=2^n,
H=pi G=ker(pi), |G|=N^2, |H|=N. Given any f:G->G, write
S_t=(f+t Id)(G), K_f=union_{t in R} S_t.

THEOREM. If any three of the four image sets S_t have cardinality N,
then |K_f| >= 3N-2. This genuinely enlarges the balanced four-image
class. More precisely, there is a power h of two dividing N such that

    |K_f| >= 4N-h-N/h-1.

The fourth image cannot have size less than N: it is unit-separated
from one of the N-point images, and the associated pair map is injective.
The fourth image also cannot have size N+1, as proved below. If it
has size greater than N, then 1<h<N and therefore

    |K_f| >= (7/2)N-3.

The latter alternative requires n>=2 and is sharp in every such
dimension for both rings. For n=5 its bound is 109.
Equality |K_f|=3N-2 forces all four images to have size N, and the
known full-hub star classification in the balanced class applies.

PROOF.
1. Replacing f by f+s Id permutes its four parameter images. We can
   arrange that the potentially nonminimum image has parameter pi.
   Translate f by -f(0). Thus write A=S_0, B=S_1, D=S_(1+pi), all
   of size N, with 0 belonging to every image.

2. The map v -> (f(v),f(v)+v) is injective, and its domain G and
   codomain A x B both have size N^2. It is therefore a bijection.
   Equivalently the difference map (a,b) -> b-a is a bijection from
   A x B onto G, and f(b-a)=a for every (a,b). Consequently

       S_pi=(1-pi)A+pi B,
       D=(1+pi)B-pi A.

3. Since 0 is in A, the second formula includes (1+pi)B in D.
   Multiplication by 1+pi is a bijection, and |D|=|B|=N, so
   D=(1+pi)B. For every a in A the translate (1+pi)B-pi a is
   contained in D and has the same size, hence equals D. Since
   pi^2=0, multiplication by (1+pi)^(-1) fixes pi a. Therefore
   B is invariant under pi A, and hence under the subgroup
   H0=span_F2(pi A) of H. Put h=|H0|.

4. Put p=|pi A| and t=|pi B|. Every nonempty pi-fibre of B is a
   union of H0-cosets, so t h <= |B|=N. Also p<=h. Applying pi
   to the surjective difference map gives pi B-pi A=H, so pt>=N.
   Thus N<=pt<=h(N/h)=N, with equality throughout. In particular

       pi A=H0,  |pi B|=m=N/h.

   The set T=pi B is a transversal for H/H0: the map
   H0 x T -> H, (x,y)->y-x, is a surjection between sets of the
   same size N. Each nonempty pi-fibre B_y has exactly h points
   and is one H0-coset. Since 0 is in B, B_0=H0.

5. For fixed x in H0 and y in T, every vector v with pi v=y-x
   has its unique difference representation b-a with pi a=x,
   pi b=y. (The pair of pi-images is unique by the preceding
   transversal property.) Hence A_x x B_y maps bijectively to
   this N-point pi-fibre of G, so |A_x|h=N and |A_x|=m.
   Moreover A_x is a transversal for H0 in that pi-fibre: two
   distinct a,a' differing by a member of H0 would, by B_y's
   H0-invariance, give two representations of the same difference.
   In particular A_0 is a transversal for H/H0 containing 0,
   and A_0 intersects H0 only in 0.

6. Define A'=(1-pi)A and B'=(1+pi)B. Both have size N, and
   A union A' union B union B' is a subset of K_f, because
   0 is in B and hence A' is in S_pi, while B'=D.

   In the pi-fibre labelled x in H0, A' is A_x translated by -x.
   If x=0 this changes nothing. If x!=0, these two transversals
   are disjoint: an intersection would give two points of A_x
   differing by the nonzero element x of H0. Thus

       A intersects A' = A_0, of size m.

   In the pi-fibre labelled y in T, B' is the H0-coset B_y
   translated by y. Its intersection with B_y is nonempty exactly
   when y belongs to H0. Since T is a transversal containing 0,
   this happens only for y=0. Therefore

       B intersects B' = H0, of size h.

   Any cross-intersection of a member of {A,A'} with a member of
   {B,B'} lies over a label in H0 intersect T={0}; in that fibre
   it is A_0 intersect H0={0}. Consequently the two unions have
   sizes 2N-m and 2N-h and intersect only at zero. Their combined
   size is exactly 4N-h-m-1, proving the more precise bound.

7. Since hm=N, h+m<=N+1, because (h-1)(m-1)>=0. Thus
   4N-h-m-1>=3N-2. Equality forces h=1 or m=1. If h=1,
   pi A={0} and A=H, which gives S_pi=H. If m=1, B=H and
   pi B={0}, which gives S_pi=(1-pi)A. Either endpoint forces
   the fourth image to have size N. If the fourth is larger,
   then h,m>=2, and h+m<=2+N/2, giving (7/2)N-3. QED.

STRICTNESS EXAMPLE (both rings, n=2).
Let e1,e2 be the standard basis of V=F2^2; write points as (x,y)
with base x and fibre coordinate y. Choose

  A_0={0,e2},  A_e1={0,e1+e2}, other A_x empty;
  B_0={0,e1},  B_e2={0,e1}, other B_x empty.

The difference map A x B -> G is bijective. Define f(v) as the
unique a in A in v=b-a. Then |S0|=|S1|=|S_(1+pi)|=4, whereas
|S_pi|=6. Its union has size11, so the theorem includes examples
strictly outside the four-balanced/minimum-four-product class.

SHARPNESS OF THE STRICT GAP IN EVERY n>=2.
Split V=F2^n as P direct-sum T, with P=<e1> and T the span of
e2,...,en. Thus |P|=2 and |T|=m=N/2. Choose a nonzero linear map
phi:T->P. Identify each residue fibre with V and choose

  A_0=T,
  A_e1={t+phi(t): t in T},
  all other A_x empty;

  B_t=P for every t in T,
  all other B_x empty.

Both A and B have N points. Their base supports P,T are complementary
subspaces, so the pair of residues for a prescribed difference is
unique. Every A-fibre is a transversal for P, and every B-fibre is P;
therefore the internal difference representation is unique too. This
proves A x B -> G is a difference tiling for both rings (any binary
carry in Z/4 merely translates the internal difference). Define f by
f(b-a)=a.

The set B is invariant under pi A=P, so S_(1+pi)=(1+pi)B has N
points. The fourth image C=(1-pi)A+pi B has only two base fibres:
C_0=T, while C_e1=V. The latter follows because phi is onto P,
so {t+phi(t):t in T}+T is all of V. Thus |C|=m+N=3N/2.
Furthermore C is contained in A union (1-pi)A: in the e1 fibre,
the graph of phi and its e1-translate partition V, and in the zero
fibre the sets both equal T. Hence K_f is exactly the four-set subset
counted in step6, and

  |K_f|=4N-2-m-1=(7/2)N-3.

This establishes sharpness of the unbalanced fourth-image gap, not
just nonemptiness of the larger class.

THE FOURTH IMAGE IS EITHER N OR AT LEAST N+2; JOINT SHARPNESS.
The preceding proof shows that each pi-fibre of C=S_pi is a union
of translates of an m-point set A_x, with translations in H, a
binary vector space. If m=1, step7 already forces |C|=N. Otherwise
m is even. The union of a set of even size m and any of its distinct
translates has even size: their intersection is invariant under the
nonzero translating element, hence is partitioned into two-point
orbits. If a union of translates has more than m points, one translate
differs from the first, and their union alone has at least m+2 points.
Thus any fibre expansion adds at least two, and |C| cannot be N+1.

This smallest possible fourth-image expansion is compatible with
equality in the sharp union gap. Take a coordinate hyperplane H0 in
V=F2^n, let t be the remaining coordinate vector, and put T={0,t}.
Choose one nonzero x0 in H0. Define A on base support H0 by

  A_x=T for x in H0 except x0,
  A_x0={0,t+x0};

and define B on the two base residues in T by B_0=B_t=H0.
As above, these are N-point sets tiling G uniquely by differences.
The first, second, and fourth images have N points. The third image
C has a two-point fibre at every x in H0 except x0, where its fibre
has four points. Hence its size is exactly N+2. Moreover C is still
contained in A union (1-pi)A: at x0 the latter union is precisely
{0,t,x0,t+x0}, and elsewhere C is just the translated A-fibre.
Consequently

  (|S0|,|S1|,|S_pi|,|S_(1+pi)|)=(N,N,N+2,N),
  |K_f|=(7/2)N-3.

For n=5 this gives (32,32,34,32) and union109, over both rings.

SCOPE.
An arbitrary Kakeya set can be represented by a containing-line
selection f with K_f contained in that set. No argument here makes
three of its parameter images have size N. Thus this is an enlarged
conditional all-dimension theorem, not the unrestricted Kakeya law.
